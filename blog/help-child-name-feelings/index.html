How to Help Your Child Name Their Feelings: 6 Simple Ideas (+ a Free Song)

When a young child is upset, they often don’t have the words for what is going on inside. So the feeling comes out as crying, shouting or stomping instead. The good news: naming feelings is a skill, and it can be learned — a little every day, through play, stories and songs.

Why naming feelings matters

Putting a feeling into words makes it smaller and easier to handle. A child who can say “I’m frustrated” is already halfway to calming down — and much more likely to ask for help instead of lashing out.

Naming feelings is also the first step to empathy: when children know what “sad” or “nervous” feels like, they start to notice those feelings in friends, too.

6 everyday ideas

  • Name your own feelings out loud: “I’m feeling a bit frustrated because the bus is late. I’ll take a deep breath.”
  • Name your child’s feeling before solving the problem: “You look disappointed — you really wanted to go to the park.”
  • Use stories: pause on a picture and ask “How do you think she feels? How can you tell?”
  • Make a feelings check-in part of the day — at breakfast or bedtime, everyone shares one feeling.
  • Go beyond happy and sad: introduce words like proud, nervous, frustrated, excited, calm, lonely.
  • Sing about it: songs give children the words in a fun, repeatable way they actually remember.

What to say when feelings get big

Stay calm and short. Get down to your child’s eye level, name the feeling (“You’re really angry”), and offer one simple tool: a deep breath, a hug, or a quiet corner. Talk about what happened later, when everyone is calm.

Remember: every feeling is okay — it’s what we do with it that matters. Children who hear this often learn that they don’t need to hide feelings, and that they can choose how to act.
